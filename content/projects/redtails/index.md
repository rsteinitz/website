## At a glance

- **What:** My PhD research on how interspecific feeding competition affects energetic condition and reproduction in wild red-tailed monkeys.
- **Why:** Small-bodied primates often navigate complex competitive landscapes, and these pressures can shape energy balance, health, and fitness.
- **How:** Combining behavioral observation, ecological data, and non-invasive energetic biomarkers from red-tailed monkeys in and around Kibale National Park, Uganda.

---

## Project overview

**How do the pressures of feeding competition become written into the biology of wild primates?**

What does it cost, physiologically, to live at the bottom of the feeding hierarchy? This project explored what it means to survive as a small primate in a crowded and highly competitive forest community.

In [Kibale National Park, Uganda](https://campuspress.yale.edu/ngogochimp/ngogo-2/), red-tailed monkeys live alongside multiple larger primate species that often compete for the same food resources. My research asked how these daily ecological pressures shape the animals experiencing them: Are some individuals consistently pushed into poorer energetic condition? Do competitors influence reproduction, stress, or long-term health?

To answer these questions, I combined behavioral observation, ecological monitoring, and non-invasive biomarkers to examine how wild red-tailed monkeys respond, both behaviorally and physiologically, to life in a complex primate community. More broadly, this work links ecological competition to individual health, energy balance, and life-history outcomes in wild primates.  

**Key Findings:** Even a single dominant competitor was enough to leave *measurable* biological costs in wild red-tailed monkeys. Simply living alongside larger primate species, even those that frequently associate peacefully with red-tailed monkeys, carried hidden physiological costs, including poorer physical condition and biomarkers associated with starvation. Across multiple forests, monkeys facing competition consistently showed signs of depleted energy reserves and physiological stress, highlighting how profoundly ecological relationships can shape physiology in ways that are not always visible from behavior alone.

## Capacity Building

This project combined ecological research with conservation, community partnership, and scientific training. By integrating non-invasive physiological methods into studies of wild primates, the work helps expand the tools available for monitoring animal health and understanding how environmental pressures affect vulnerable species living in increasingly fragmented habitats.

The project also created direct local impacts through long-term collaboration with communities surrounding the Rwengobe forest fragment in Uganda, where research activities supported habitat preservation through sustainable, research-based income. In parallel, the project provided hands-on training and mentorship to a diverse group of emerging scientists. This included mentoring **2 Ugandan undergraduate students** through senior thesis projects conducted as part of the research; providing project design and management experience to **2 American post-baccalaureate researchers** prior to graduate school (both of whom later continued into graduate programs), and training **11 American undergraduate students**, most from groups historically underrepresented in STEM. Trainees gained experience in behavioral observation, ecological field methods, sample collection, and physiological analysis, with several continuing into graduate education and wildlife research careers.
